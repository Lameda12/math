Four thousand problems.
372 results.

This repository holds the mathematics an unreleased OpenAI model wrote when it was posed about 4,000 open research problems: 722 manuscripts in 372 result families, 235 of them with a Lean formalization of at least part of the claim. This is a walk through it, one directory at a time, each drawn in ascii.

  • 722 manuscripts
  • 372 result families
  • 34,815 pages
  • 235 with Lean
  • 17 fields

Your system asks for less motion, so the pictures hold still.

The README describes the procedure. Most results came from the same fixed process, using on average three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking compute each. Aggregating the output into families and manuscripts, and requiring an appropriate level of significance, produced the catalogue. The exceptions it names are the work on a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function and the Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties; the write-up of the Re(s) > 11/12 zero-free region was also edited by people for readability.

The README is plain about status: the results are at different stages of verification, not all have Lean formalizations, and some of the unformalized ones could have issues.

Checking the statement, not just the proof

A formal proof is only as good as the statement it proves. Each Comparator challenge restates one theorem on its own, over plain Mathlib, ending in sorry. A JSON file beside it names the module that holds the proof, the theorem it has to supply, and the only axioms that proof may rest on.

This one is result 003, the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. Its proof lives in lean/OAI/NumberTheory/DirichletL: 2,926 files and 486,558 lines for a statement that fits on two.

What that statement says

The Riemann hypothesis would put every nontrivial zero of ζ on the line Re s = ½. This result proves something weaker but new: no zeros at all to the right of 7/8. Per its scope note, the Lean formalization covers ζ, every Dirichlet L-function uniformly in the modulus, and finite-order Hecke L-functions over ℚ(√−3).

Ten summaries of the reasoning

For ten results the release includes an abridged summary of how the model got there. They mostly paraphrase, but in places they quote the raw reasoning verbatim. Two such excerpts from the Mézard–Parisi summary:

Aha recursive structure: overfitting shared variable can simulate original model at smaller scale.verbatim, mezard-parisi-formula.tex, §1
Great! Need carefully prove average error little with shifted depths not rareverbatim, mezard-parisi-formula.tex, §2

The second marks the turn the summary calls shifting branching depths: moving every internal branching depth of a target tree down one level, so each new branching vertex pays a small fresh factor.
